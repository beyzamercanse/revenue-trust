# Case study: why did June revenue fall?

Simulated e-commerce scenario (seed 41). The numbers below come straight from `dashboard/data.json` after a clean pipeline run.

## Short answer

Net revenue fell from **£102,564.44** in May to **£90,290.90** in June: **−£12,273.54 (−11.97%)**. About £3.4k of that is only June being a day shorter; per calendar day the decline is 9.03%. Almost all of the rest is Mobile: fewer paid orders than its own run-rate, and refunds more than three times higher than in May. My recommendation is to investigate mobile checkout and refunds before touching acquisition spend.

## How the drop breaks down

At total level:

| Component | June minus May |
|---|---:|
| Paid-order volume (1,181 → 1,077 orders) | −£9,303.97 |
| Average paid order value | +£489.57 |
| Refund postings (£3,089.28 → £6,548.42) | −£3,459.14 |
| **Total** | **−£12,273.54** |

That bridge uses May's average order value for the volume effect and puts the interaction in the order-value line. It says *how* revenue fell, but not *where*, so I split it by channel and separated the calendar effect out of volume:

| Channel | Calendar | Volume vs run-rate | Order value | Refunds | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Mobile | −£889.52 | −£7,145.62 | −£834.89 | −£2,398.54 | **−£11,268.57** |
| Web | −£1,711.80 | −£1,896.32 | +£723.91 | −£975.82 | **−£3,860.03** |
| Marketplace | −£806.86 | +£3,072.51 | +£674.19 | −£84.78 | **+£2,855.06** |
| **Total** | −£3,408.18 | −£5,969.43 | +£563.21 | −£3,459.14 | **−£12,273.54** |

Mobile accounts for 92% of the net change. Its paid orders went from 302 to 214, and refund postings from £934.53 to £3,333.07. Web declined too, partly because of the shorter month. Marketplace grew, and in the total it hides part of the Mobile problem.

These are arithmetic contributions. They show where to look; they don't prove a cause.

## Can Finance trust it?

- 15,248 delivered events → 14,770 unique records. Retried deliveries can't be counted twice.
- 7,089 orders after keeping the latest version of each; 6,688 of them paid.
- 413 refunds accepted, 3 quarantined (missing order, non-positive amount, refund larger than the payment).
- Modelled net revenue matches the ledger on all 181 days, to the penny.
- 12 automated tests cover reruns, late revisions, conflicting versions, malformed dates, invalid refunds, ledger rollback and the bridge arithmetic.

One caveat: the ledger in this scenario is generated by the same process as the orders. With real data, the reconciliation target should be the ledger Finance owns.

## What I'd do next

1. **Mobile checkout.** Join payment-attempt and release logs, then measure paid completions per payment attempt by device and payment method. The current data has no attempts, so the drop in orders can't be separated from a drop in traffic.
2. **Mobile refunds.** Pull refund reasons and products. Separate returns from duplicate charges and payment reversals. Look at refund probability by order cohort once the observation window is complete.
3. **Keep the gates.** Version handling, quarantine and ledger reconciliation stay as publication blockers. Unresolved refunds get an owner and an age, and restatements after late corrections are logged.

Any of those fixes would need its own test or a defensible before/after design to claim an effect on revenue.
